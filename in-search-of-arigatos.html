In Search of
Arigatos..

A small elevator ritual in Tokyo, and the first time I realised that even standing beside a lift button could have a social meaning.

A Tokyo elevator, imagined through the small rituals I was beginning to notice.
Day 2
Place Tokyo
Theme Everyday etiquette
Written 2026

Initial days to college were always a little tidy and sharp. Remembering which lifts to take, which route to follow, which stairs to find — everything seemed difficult.

Like a detective, I had to remember the signs. Even the signages initially looked like Egyptian walls. I started memorising the different anime figures I saw along the way. Lifts were no different; there were so many of them to remember.

One random day, as usual, happy to have found the right lift, I stepped in.

As we reached our floor, the person standing by the panel held the door open while everyone filed out. One by one, they stepped past him, each person giving a small bow and a soft “arigato gozaimashita.”

I also stepped out, without a sign or gratitude.

Rude me.

Actually, I didn't know arigato — the Japanese way of saying “thank you” — either. Pardon my arrogance.

The second day, the same thing happened. Same spot. Someone to press the button. Same bow. Same showering of arigatos.

Then I noticed: the guy was no random. He mostly entered at the end, and he kept holding the button until all the older people had filed out.

Nobody had assigned him the job. Nobody had explained it to me. Yet somehow, everybody seemed to know.

I did some ChatGPT.

It turned out that I wasn't watching mere politeness. There is a broader Japanese etiquette of kamiza and shimoza — literally the “upper seat” and “lower seat”.

In an elevator, the position near the controls is generally treated as the shimoza, the working position. The more senior person or guest is expected to stand farther inside, away from the entrance. The person at the panel operates the buttons and holds the door.

The same idea can appear in taxis, meeting rooms and other seating arrangements. It is less about a rigid rulebook and more about recognising who should be made comfortable — and who should take responsibility for the small practical tasks.

A small correction to my first impression
The elevator custom is better understood as an application of kamiza-shimoza etiquette, rather than a universal rule that the youngest person must always operate every elevator. Context, workplace culture and practicality still matter.

Nobody assigns this job. Nobody explains it. No one insists.

The nice one seems to know — and quietly abide by it.

And then there were the little emergency provisions inside some elevators. I noticed what looked like a small folded seat or cabinet near the back and initially wondered whether it was meant for someone to sit during the ride.

It wasn't.

In some buildings, these are emergency disaster chairs or cabinets, stocked with supplies such as water and emergency toilet facilities for people who may become trapped inside an elevator after a disaster.

A country accustomed to preparing for earthquakes had thought about something as specific as being stuck inside a lift.

They really have thought of everything.

By the next week, having learned all about the theory of being the lift operator, I was ready to get some bows and arigato gozaimashita.

Yeah, it is slightly difficult to get some attention from them otherwise.

These days, my biggest challenge is finding a lift where I'm still the youngest person by the panel.

I clearly showed up in this country a few years too late.

Now I get to “operate” maybe once a week — like a benched player brought in for a nostalgia run.
